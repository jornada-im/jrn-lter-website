Drylands2 Model Equations
=========================

Based in part upon Rietkerk et al. (2002) and Rietkerk et al. (1997)

The processes are:

- surface water ponding and diffusion,
- infiltration,
- evaporation from the surface soil layer,
- drainage from subsurface soil layers,
- soil water uptake by plant roots,
- soil water lateral diffusion,
- plant-available nitrogen cycling,
- plant growth,
- plant mortality.

The pools of biomass and water and plant-available nitrogen are modified
by PDEs: Change in surface water, change in soil water, change in N,
change in plant biomass, and optionally, surface water convection off
mounds.

Soil Infiltration Rate (mm d\ :sup:`-1`)
----------------------------------------

Infiltration is assumed to occur homogeneously throughout the soil
depth, and at a rate much less than one day. This assumption of
approximately constant hydraulic conductivity is used within the context
of the daily timestep of the model. The monsoon rain events mostly occur
within one hour (Keller et al, 2023), and runoff generally is not
present for most rain events (< 10 mm/event). The histogram below shows
percentages for 5 mm/day intervals for JRN site C-SAND; approximately
85% of rain events are < 10 mm in amount.

Infiltration is estimated using a Michaelis-Menten equation (see
Rietkirk et al. (2002), Walter et al. (1981)) as a function of surface
water and plant biomass density:

.. math:: {I = {O \times \alpha}}\frac{{P + k_{2}}W_{0}}{P + k_{2}}

Discussion of Infiltration Equation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

- From Walker et al (1981): Because semi-arid savanna is a water-limited
  system, any reduction in the rate of water infiltration into the soil
  is critical. On medium to heavy-textured soils, infiltration decreases
  as the surface pores become sealed ('capping') and soil erodes, and
  capping and erosion increase in rate as grass-cover and litter-cover
  decline. In Rhodesian grasslands and savanna, sites without litter
  have an infiltration rate into a loamy savanna soil of 1.4 mm/minute,
  or approximately 2000 mm/day (Kelly & Walker, 1976, tables 8 and 9);
  litter increases the infiltration rate to about ten times.
- On the other hand, woody plants can influence the water content of the
  subsoil. The canopy intercepts rain and funnels it down the stem and
  into the soil at the tree base. Pressland (1973), working in an
  Australian savanna, has recorded a six-fold increase in the amount of
  water percolating into the soil below trees as compared to open areas,
  and Reynolds (1966) has noted a similar phenomenon in a British
  woodland. Further means by which water penetrates preferentially to
  the subsoil are the quick-flow channels or tongues of preferred flow
  associated with wetting front instability.
- In the absence of any grass there will still be some infiltration
  (i\ :sub:`0`). This minimum is likely to be about 10% of the maximum
  (Kelly & Walker, 1976). The rate of infiltration will initially
  increase rapidly as G increases and then approach the maximum
  asymptotically with increasing G, as depicted in Fig. 1.

|image3|

Crust:

- Generally on sandy soils, mature biologic soil crust inhibits
  infiltration relative to bare soil (Warren, 2014).

- In dune sand and sandy loess soils, crust inhibits infiltration
  relative to scraped soil (Eldridge et al., 2000).
- In calcareous SL and SiL textured soils, infiltration is higher in
  crust vs scraped soil (Chamizo et al, 2016, fig. 2).
- Infiltration can be higher in rugose crust in arid drylands relative
  to non-crust surfaces due aggregation and crust biology, but the data
  is mixed (Belnap, 2006).
- The Jornada LTER NPP sites (non-playa) are relatively coarse textured
  (LS to SL), with the finest being SCL (site FLCE).

Evaporation (mm d\ :sup:`-1`)
-----------------------------

The amount of actual evaporation is assumed to generally decrease as
biomass density increases (CITE).

Evaporation is from the surface soil layer only. Actual evaporation is
estimated using the inverse generalized Gompertz equation with potential
bare soil evaporation and biomass as controlling parameters:

.. math:: {E_{\mathit{est}} = E_{\mathit{pot}}}\left( {1.0 - b^{{- c}b^{{- E_{\mathit{mr}}}P}}} \right)

+--------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
| E\ :sub:`pot` (mm) | Daily potential evaporation for bare soil, or   |
|                    | evaporation demand; from F(temperature) or      |
|                    | field measurements. This is the maximum         |
|                    | asymptote. Potential evaporation should not     |
|                    | exceed available soil water in the surface      |
|                    | layer (W), and should not be less than the      |
|                    | minimum daily potential evaporation under the   |
|                    | plant canopy (E\ :sub:`0`).                     |
+--------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
| b                  | Changes the relative lengths of the minimum and |
|                    | maximum asymptotes; b > e increases the length  |
|                    | of the minimum asymptote. This value could be   |
|                    | proportional to the radius of a shrub's canopy, |
|                    | or the influence of aboveground biomass density |
|                    | upon limiting bare soil evaporation.            |
+--------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
| c                  | Sets the displacement along the x-axis of the   |
|                    | inflection point; smaller values shift the      |
|                    | curve left.                                     |
+--------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
| E\ :sub:`mr`       | Slope of the curve at the inflection point;     |
|                    | proportional to the maximum change in           |
|                    | evaporation with biomass. This could reflect    |
|                    | the change in shading or protection from wind   |
|                    | at the edge of the biomass.                     |
|                    | E.g., E\ :sub:`mr` = (E\ :sub:`maximum` –       |
|                    | E\ :sub:`minimum`) / (Max. potential biomass).  |
+--------------------+-------------------------------------------------+

Grasses and shrubs will have different values for E\ :sub:`mr` and
E\ :sub:`0`, and possibly b and c.

Discussion of Evaporation Equation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

From Breshears et al (1998):

- Mean monthly soil temperature in intercanopy patches exceeded that in
  canopy patches by at least 1 C from April through August, while the
  converse was true from October through February.
- During late spring and summer (May–August), the integrated difference
  in soil water potential between the two patch types over a day was
  positive, indicating that soil evaporation was greater in intercanopy
  than canopy patches. In contrast, during fall and winter months, the
  difference between intercanopy and canopy soil water potential was
  negative (fig. 5), indicating that soil evaporation was less in
  intercanopy than in canopy patches.
- Kelly & Walker (1976) show that within the top 40 cm of soil, the soil
  dries rapidly; “soil moisture content did not exceed 5% for more than
  five weeks after a single wet period.”

Soil water loss from drainage (mm d\ :sup:`-1`)
-----------------------------------------------

Soil water drainage from the bottom of the soil profile is assumed to
occur only from the soil layers below the surface layer. The drainage
amount is a constant fraction of soil water content.

The amount of drainage cannot reduce the soil water content below the
field capacity of the soil layer. Field capacity (see class *Constants*)
is defined as the fraction of the soil layer thickness (mm),
representing the volume of soil water remaining after gravity drainage
is essentially complete.

.. math:: W_{L} = {r_{W} \times W}

Resource Drainage
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

For resources that are associated with soil water, they should drain in
proportion to their relative amount. For soil N, the amount of N within
the soil layer is assumed to be all water-soluable; then the amount
drained is proportional to the fraction of soil water drained.

**Nitrogen**
------------

dN = external N inputs + N from plant decomposition - plant N uptake -
physical removal of N

Soil nitrogen inputs considered here are atmospheric deposition, aeolian
deposition, decomposition of plant material, and browser urine/feces.
Currently, atmospheric deposition and plant material input are
implemented. Soil nitrogen removals include plant uptake, browsing,
water and aeolian erosion, N volatization, leaching, and lateral
dispersion. Currently, plant uptake and leaching are implemented.

The change in plant-available soil nitrogen is from Rietkerk et al
(1997), eq. 7:

.. math:: {\frac{\mathit{dN}}{\mathit{dt}} = {N_{\mathit{input}} - c_{N}}}{P - r_{N}}N

The aboveground biomass growth fraction from plant-available soil
nitrogen is associated with plant water uptake (Rietkerk et al, 1997,
eq. 8):

.. math:: {g_{N} = {g_{N,\mathit{\max}} \times \frac{N}{N + k_{1}}}},\mspace{108mu}{\mathit{units} = g}{C/g}{C_{\mathit{biomass}}/\mathit{day}}

The soil N uptake due to that biomass increase, as the fraction of plant
biomass (from Rietkerk et al, 1997, eq. 9):

.. math:: {c_{N,\mathit{up}} = {c_{N,\mathit{\max}} \times \frac{N}{N + k_{1}}}},\mspace{108mu}{\mathit{units} = g}{N/g}{C_{\mathit{biomass}}/\mathit{day}}

Plant mortality contribution to soil N is provided by the mortality rate
and the C:N of the plant material:

.. math:: {d_{N,i} = {{({P_{i} \times d_{i}})}/R_{i}}},\mspace{72mu}{R_{i} = C}:N\mathit{of}\mathit{plant}\mathit{part}i;\mspace{72mu}{\mathit{units} = g}{N/\mathit{day}}

Then, the total plant contribution to change in soil N is:

.. math:: c_{N} = {c_{N,\mathit{up}} - {\sum\limits_{i}d_{N,i}}}

Physical processes cause loss of soil N, including leaching, erosion,
volatization, and lateral dispersion with surface and soil water.
Currently only leaching is implemented. Leaching is proportional to soil
water drainage.

Rietkerk et al, 1997 (eq. 10) proposed the following Michaelis-Menten
equation for water and wind erosion loss as a function of aboveground
biomass. In the case of monsoon events, k2 may need to be a function of
event intensity (both rain and wind).

.. math:: r_{N} = {r_{N,\mathit{\max}} \times \frac{k_{2}}{k_{2} + P}}

N Implementation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

C:N values: Each plant part, wood, shoot and leaf, coarse roots and fine
roots, will have a different C:N ratio. Here assume shrubs have all four
parts, and grasses have shoots and fine roots.

Each plant species will have an N pool associated with the plant
biomass, since the parameters used in the equations above
(g\ :sub:`N,max`, k\ :sub:`1`, c\ :sub:`N,max`, C:N) are plant-specific.
Parameter c\ :sub:`N,max` is specified in the configuration. Parameter
g\ :sub:`N,max` is assumed to be the maximum daily new biomass as
fraction of current biomass, i.e., configuration parameter r\ :sub:`max`
(see **Plant Growth**).

Source of N inputs: As with precipitation, N input can be a constant
daily value, or read from a data file.

Future considerations:

- Implement soil N pools per soil layer; currently there is one N pool
  for the entire soil depth.
- Add a lateral dispersion term to the change in N, e.g.,
  *D*\ :sub:`N`\ *\*LN/x*\ :sup:`2`. N amount is proportional to surface
  and soil water dispersion (amounts may be very small - important?)
- Add water and aeolian erosion as the r\ :sub:`N` term. (Amounts may be
  very small - important?)
- Add volatization to r\ :sub:`N` term. Volatization is a function of
  near-surface N concentration and soil temperature. (Independent of
  biomass?)
- Add to c\ :sub:`N` a term for removal of biomass due to browsing.

For multiple plant species, the terms c\ :sub:`N`, d, and d\ :sub:`N`
are specific for each species. This modifies the equation of change in N
to be:

.. math:: {\frac{\mathit{dN}}{\mathit{dt}} = {N_{\mathit{input}} - {\sum\limits_{p}\left( {c_{N}{{(P_{p})} \times P_{p}}} \right)} - r_{N}}}N

\ where p is the index to the plant array.

Temperature Effect Upon Growth
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Parameterization of a generalized Poisson density function curve to
simulate temperature effect on growth (Parton and Innis, 1972):

.. math:: f = \frac{b - T}{b - a}

\ Position of temperature T on the interval [a, b], as a fraction.

.. math:: \mathit{gpdf} = {f^{c} \times e^{({\frac{c}{d}{({1 - f^{d}})}})}}

\ Poisson density function returning a value on [0, 1] corresponding to
T.

| Where:
| a = Optimum temperature for production; the peak of the curve.
| b = Maximum temperature for production; larger values = broader bell
  shape, and production is less sensitive to soil temperature.
| c = Spread of the bell curve; smaller values = broader bell shape, and
  production is less sensitive to soil temperature.
| d = Left curve shape; smaller values = more production at lower
  temperatures.

Mortality
---------

The model uses a relatively simple biomass mortality algorithm to relate
mortality fraction to plant water stress. The main variables used to
approximate water stress are plant soil water demand, plant-available
soil water content, and duration of the water stress. Much of the
literature emphasizes thresholds which determine changes in the
mortality rate. For modeling, a continuous function allows for a simpler
algorithmic representation than thresholds. Each plant species has it's
own mortality parameterization and hence rate calculation. The time
scale in the model is daily for both soil moisture and biomass
mortality.

Water stress here is defined as plant demand for soil water beyond what
is available. Cumulative water stress as considered here is unmet plant
demand for soil water over a fixed time period. From Fu et al. (2002):

The critical soil moisture (SM) threshold (Θ\ :sub:`crit`) of plant
water stress occurs near the point when evapotranspiration becomes
SM-limited. ...when SM is lower than Θ\ :sub:`crit`, the capacity of
plants to extract soil water by roots and xylem transport becomes
progressively reduced. Under this SM-­limited regime, evapotranspiration
decreases with decreasing SM until the wilting point, where
transpiration is blocked because of full stomatal closure, with little
or no further change in EF with SM decrease.

The physiological effects of water stress that produce mortality are not
directly addressed in this modeling approach.

Mortality vs Water Stress
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The relationship between water stress (and resulting mortality) and soil
water content is often defined as one or more thresholds (see Fu et al,
2022, figures 1B and 1C and equation 1). Barkaoui and Volaire (2023) use
logistic function to fit survival rate vs. SWC and plant water content
(see their eq. 1, and figure 3b). The form of the equation is:

*S = 1 / (1 + e*\ :sup:`-(B0-B1*SWC)`\ *)*

where

|  *B0* = SWC at the logistic function inflection point.
| *B1* = Maximum rate of change of survival.
| *SWC* = soil water content

Then the mortality rate is *1-S*. In that equation, *SWC* can be
replaced by excess demand of soil water content by plants:

*M = 1 / (1 + e*\ :sup:`(B0-B1\*\ E\ )`\ *)*

where

*E* = Difference of plant water demand - available soil water as a
fraction of soil water content.

The script **plot-mortality.m** provides graphs of *M* vs. *E* for
varying *B0*.

Implementation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Each plant species specifies the parameters *B0*, *B1*, and the length
of the stress period defining the plant's response to water stress. The
stress period is the number of days prior to the current simulation day,
and the length is constant. Each day's value of *E* is added to the
stress period, dropping the oldest value, and the sum is the resulting
*E* for the stress period.

The daily background rate is also specified for each species, and is
added to the water stress calculation.

Some considerations not implemented:
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

- Effect of high temperatures upon water stress.
- Increase in root mass with drought stress (Wach and Skowron, 2022).
- Change in water use efficiency with drought stress (Wach and Skowron,
  2022).

- For multiple plant species, this equation is applied separately to
  each. However, the growth function includes an interspecies
  competition effect.

Propagation
~~~~~~~~~~~

From Breshears et al (1998):

- The differences in soil temperature between the patch types directly
  affect biological processes such as germination for both woody and
  herbaceous species (Jordan and Haferkamp 1989; Fulbright et al. 1995;
  Roundy and Biedenbender 1996). For example, germination rates for
  *Pinus edulis* at a soil temperature of 30C are more than four times
  less than at 20C (Floyd 1983).
- The amount of plant-available water, in turn, affects germination
  percentages and rates of herbaceous (Briske and Wilson 1976; Everett
  and Sharrow 1985; Aguilera and Lauenroth 1995) and woody (Daubenmire
  1943; Meagher 1943) species. Small differences in soil moisture may
  have disproportionately greater effects on germination (Lauenroth et
  al. 1987).
